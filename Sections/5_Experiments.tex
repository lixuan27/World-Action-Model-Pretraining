\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\Paragraph{Evaluation suites.}
Two closed-loop battlegrounds carry the study, and \tref{tab:suites} states their
protocols. The first is a single-arm tabletop benchmark with four task families
that separate spatial reasoning, object identity, goal specification, and
long-horizon composition, together with a perturbation suite over the same tasks
that varies camera placement, robot initial state, language phrasing, lighting,
background, sensor noise, and object layout along seven independent axes
\citep{liu2023libero,liberoplus}. The second is a bimanual benchmark of fifty
tasks with a clean split and a domain-randomised split that alters table height,
clutter, background, and lighting, plus two arm types that appear only at
evaluation time \citep{chen2025robotwin2}. The first battleground measures
precision and perturbation robustness on one embodiment, and the second measures
in-distribution precision, layout generalisation, and embodiment transfer.
Neither corpus enters pretraining.

\Paragraph{Model and budget.}
Every arm uses the world stream and the action stream of \sref{sec:streams} at
their stated sizes. The coupling axis changes one attention mask and one
stop-gradient, so its arms are parameter-matched by construction; the only arm
with a different parameter count is the single-stream structural control, which
concatenates the action tokens into the video sequence and removes the second
stream. Fine-tuning runs consume twenty thousand optimiser steps at sixteen
windows per device. Pretraining runs share one step budget across recipes, so a
recipe comparison separates corpus composition from corpus size. Compute is a
per-user allocation of sixteen accelerators with eighty gigabytes each, with
short jobs permitted to overflow to forty-eight under a preemptible queue, which
sets the pretraining scale recorded in \tref{tab:corpus}.

\Paragraph{Protocol, floors, and statistics.}
Closed-loop scores come from the official client of each benchmark driven through
the policy server of \sref{sec:procedure}, with fifty trials per task, a fixed
initial-state seed, and the evaluation seed recorded in the result file. Main
tables report the mean over three training seeds. Every comparison between two
arms is step-matched, uses the same data manifest and the same seed set, and is
tested with a paired bootstrap over tasks at the ninety-five percent level; a
difference counts as established when the interval excludes zero. Each
representation measurement declares its degenerate floors in advance, and a
measurement that fails to clear its floors is reported as such. Every run writes
a metrics file and a manifest holding the resolved configuration, the git commit,
and the data fingerprint, and the experiment ledger binds each number in this
section to one such file.

\subsection{Reference Regime and Mainline Reproduction}
\label{sec:mainline}

The first question is whether joint flow matching on a pretrained video prior
reaches the performance regime that a released world-action implementation
occupies on these suites, because a mechanism study is only informative once the
mainline arm is competitive. The reference values in \tref{tab:mainline} are
reproduced verbatim from that public release and from the public reports of three
vision-language-action policies, and they define the regime rather than a target
that this work claims.

One difference between the rows governs how the table is read. Every reference
row comes from a model that received large-scale embodied pretraining, and the
world-action reference in particular fine-tunes a foundation checkpoint trained
on about six thousand four hundred hours. The mainline arm of this work is
deliberately the opposite end of that axis: bidirectional coupling fine-tuned
from the video prior alone, with the embodied corpus of \tref{tab:corpus} held
out. Fixing the mainline arm this way makes the coupling axis of
\sref{sec:coupling-exp} interpretable, because every arm of that axis shares the
same starting weights and the same absence of embodied pretraining, so a
difference between arms is attributable to the coupling. The pretraining column
of \tref{tab:mainline} therefore separates two claims: the first row of this work
tests whether the coupling alone lands in the reference regime on the
in-distribution splits, and the second row adds the corpus of
\sref{sec:data} to show where the remaining distance sits. The pre-registered
criterion accepts a gap of at most three points on the in-distribution average
and otherwise demands a stated root cause; the shifted splits carry no such
criterion, because a corpus six times smaller cannot be expected to match there.

\subsection{Joint Modelling Against Auxiliary Action Supervision}
\label{sec:coupling-exp}

The central comparison sweeps the coupling axis of \sref{sec:coupling} one
degree of freedom at a time. Six arms differ only in the joint attention mask
and one stop-gradient, so parameters, data, optimiser, and seeds are identical
across them, and a seventh arm replaces the two-stream construction with a
single stream that concatenates action tokens into the video sequence. Two
further arms add the boundary-ray mass of \sref{sec:plane} on top of the
bidirectional setting, which tests whether training the two conditional slices
explicitly changes the joint model. The comparison is designed to separate two
hypotheses that a benchmark average alone confounds: the auxiliary hypothesis,
realised by letting the action stream read a world representation it cannot
shape, and the joint hypothesis, realised by letting the action objective shape
that representation.

\subsection{What the Coupling Changes Inside the Representation}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\Paragraph{Linear decodability.}
The world-clamped ray of \sref{sec:paths} turns the trained model into an
inverse dynamics model, and a linear probe on the hidden states of the world
stream measures the same property without running the sampler. Every arm of the
coupling axis is frozen, the hidden state of each world layer is read, and one
ridge regression per layer predicts the future action chunk and the future
consequence trajectory. Two floors bound the measurement: a randomly initialised
backbone of the same shape, which gives the score attainable from architecture
alone, and a shuffled-label probe, which gives the score attainable from the
marginal statistics of the target.

\Paragraph{Counterfactual controllability.}
The action-clamped ray asks whether the generated future follows the action
rather than merely correlating with it. The action tokens are clamped to four
different chunks and the world stream is denoised to completion, and the
displacement of the manipulator keypoints in the generated future is compared
with the displacement the clamped chunk implies. The true chunk is the
condition of interest, and a chunk drawn from a different episode, a zero chunk,
and a time-shuffled chunk are the three floors. A passive world model has no
action input at all and therefore sits at the uncontrollable end of the scale.

\tref{tab:probe} reports the linear probes and \fref{fig:probe} reports them per
layer.

\subsection{Corpus Composition and Pretraining Scale}
\label{sec:data}

Four pretraining recipes share one step budget and one fine-tuning protocol, so
the comparison isolates what each kind of data contributes. The first recipe
skips pretraining and fine-tunes the video prior directly, which reproduces the
mainline arm and fixes the reference point of the section. The second pretrains
on the robot sources of \tref{tab:corpus} alone, where both terms of the
objective are supervised. The third adds the egocentric human sources with an
empty action mask, so those windows supervise the world term alone. The fourth
adds the consequence stream on the egocentric sources, so the same action-free
footage supervises the consequence term as well and grounds the action side of
the model without any action label. A separate ladder holds the recipe fixed and
varies the corpus fraction over one tenth, three tenths, and the whole, which
separates the effect of composition from the effect of volume. The additional
column records the action loss at the first fine-tuning step, which reads how
much of the downstream action mapping the pretrained model already carries.

\tref{tab:data} reports the recipes and the ladder, and \fref{fig:scale} plots

\subsection{Out of Distribution and Transfer}
\label{sec:ood}

The distribution shift study crosses the coupling with the corpus. Three
representation settings appear, namely a passive world model with a policy
readout trained on top of it, the independent-objective arm, and the
bidirectional arm, and each appears with and without the consequence pretraining
recipe. Four kinds of shift are measured on the same checkpoints: the seven
perturbation axes of the single-arm suite, the domain-randomised split of the
bimanual suite, and two arm types that appear only at evaluation time. The
in-distribution column travels with every out-of-distribution column, because a
robustness claim only means something once the in-distribution score is stated
next to it. The pre-registered criterion asks whether pretraining and coupling
interact superadditively on the shifted splits. Absolute values on those splits
belong to the corpus scale of \tref{tab:corpus}, so they compare between the rows
of this table and stay below the reference row of \tref{tab:mainline}, which
carries a corpus six times larger.

\subsection{Consequence Grounding}
\label{sec:conseq}

Three questions close the study, and all three run on one trained model. The
first asks whether the two levels of grounding carry complementary information,
which the clamped rays of \sref{sec:paths} answer by conditioning the world
stream on the action chunk, on the consequence chunk, and on both, then measuring
the fidelity of the generated future together with the follow rate of
\tref{tab:counterfactual}. The second asks whether the consequence
representation is shared across embodiments, which a probe trained on one
embodiment and evaluated on a held-out one answers against a cross-embodiment
mean floor. The third asks whether consequence prediction serves as a quality
probe for the representation, which the ordering of arms in the consequence
columns of \tref{tab:probe} already reports.

\tref{tab:conseq} reports the conditioning comparison and the cross-embodiment
probe, and \fref{fig:conseq} places the conditioning settings side by side.

\subsection{Failure Modes}
\label{sec:failure}

The study states in advance the conditions under which the coupling stops
paying, and three stresses probe them. The first reduces the pretraining corpus
along the ladder of \tref{tab:data}, which asks how much data the coupling needs
before it separates from the independent-objective arm. The second restricts the
corpus to a subset whose action space is joint angles and whose camera is fixed,
which decouples the action from its visual effect and therefore removes the
signal the coupling is supposed to exploit. The third restricts the exchange to
one end of the backbone, which asks where in depth the grounding is formed. Each
stress reports the coupling gain, defined as the gap between the bidirectional
arm and the independent-objective arm on the shifted split, because that gap is
the quantity the whole study is about.

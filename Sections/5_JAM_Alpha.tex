\section{\sysa: Scaling the Selected Configuration}
\label{sec:alpha}

\subsection{Pretraining Corpus and Recipe}
\label{sec:corpus}

\Paragraph{Corpus.}
The pretraining corpus combines human egocentric footage with robot
trajectories collected on real hardware and in simulation.
\tref{tab:corpus} gives its composition. Thirty percent of the footage carries
human hands and no action label, forty percent carries teleoperated real robot
trajectories across gripper and dexterous-hand platforms, and thirty percent
carries simulated trajectories from the benchmark generators. Every source is
read through the same window sampler at thirty-three raw frames with a video
stride of four, and every source writes into the unified eighty-slot action
layout through its own index map with per-dimension supervision masks. Sources
with heterogeneous physical units receive per-source quantile normalisation, and
the human component carries a fully masked action target so it contributes to the
video term alone.

\Paragraph{Model and recipe.}
\sysa{} instantiates exactly the configuration the study selects: the dual system
with joint self-attention over the Wan2.2 text-and-image-to-video backbone at
five billion parameters, the reconstructive latent of that backbone's
autoencoder, the mutual visibility mode, and first-frame causal video
self-visibility. Pretraining co-trains the human and robot components in one
mixture with proportional sampling by window count, runs for one hundred and
fifty thousand optimiser steps at a global batch of one thousand and
twenty-four windows on sixty-four devices with eighty gigabytes each, and
otherwise uses the optimiser settings of \sref{sec:protocol}. Every downstream
result below fine-tunes this single checkpoint for thirty thousand steps on eight
devices using the target platform's own data, changing nothing else.

\Paragraph{Evaluation harness.}
Every evaluation runs the policy behind one server that speaks a fixed
observation and action protocol, and each benchmark provides a client in its own
environment. The client sends camera images, the proprioceptive state and the
instruction, and it receives an action chunk in the platform's native units after
the framework inverts the unified layout and the normalisation. Baseline numbers
come from the public reports of the systems named in each table. Nine simulated
suites and three physical platforms are covered.

\subsection{Simulated Benchmarks}
\label{sec:sim}

\Paragraph{Setup.}
Nine suites cover complementary competences. LIBERO scores four task families
that separate spatial reasoning, object identity, goal specification and
long-horizon composition, with five hundred trials per family. LIBERO-plus
scores the same policy under seven perturbation categories that alter camera
placement, robot initial state, language phrasing, lighting, background, sensor
noise and object layout. RoboTwin 2.0 scores fifty bimanual tasks under two
fine-tuning protocols, one that trains on clean demonstrations only and probes
the randomized suite as an out-of-distribution test, and one that trains on both
and probes each suite in distribution. RoboCasa365 scores atomic kitchen skills
together with composite tasks whose object sets are seen and unseen. RoboCasa GR1
scores humanoid tabletop tasks. VLABench scores ten primitive tasks across five
evaluation tracks and reports success rate together with a progress score and an
intention score. EBench scores generalist tabletop, pick-and-place and
long-horizon tasks and reports success rate together with a progress score.
RoboDojo scores six task groups spanning generalization, precision, long horizon,
memory and open-ended instruction, and reports success rate together with a
progress score.
\fref{fig:radar} places every suite on one axis. The detailed comparisons follow
in \tref{tab:libero} for LIBERO, \tref{tab:liberoplus} for LIBERO-plus,
\tref{tab:robotwin} for RoboTwin 2.0, \tref{tab:robocasa} for the two RoboCasa
suites, \tref{tab:vlabench} for VLABench, \tref{tab:ebench} for EBench and
\tref{tab:robodojo} for RoboDojo.

\subsection{Physical Platforms}
\label{sec:real}

\Paragraph{Setup.}
Three physical platforms receive their own fine-tuning run from the same
pretrained checkpoint. The first is a single-arm Franka Panda with a parallel
gripper, one head camera and one wrist camera, teleoperated at fifteen hertz. The
second is a twenty-four degree-of-freedom dexterous hand mounted on a seven-axis
arm with one head camera and one wrist camera. The third pools three bimanual
arm types under one task suite, and each arm type carries its own fine-tuned
variant. Every platform is evaluated by rolling out the policy from randomised
initial object placements with a fixed step limit, and a trial counts as a
success when the task's completion predicate holds at the end of the rollout.
Twenty trials per task are run on the single-arm platform, fifteen on the
dexterous hand and twenty-five on each bimanual task. The comparison reports the
strongest prior world-action model where a published result on that platform
class exists, together with a vision-language-action reference fine-tuned on the
identical data. \tref{tab:real} gives the per-task breakdown.

\subsection{Deployment Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

\Paragraph{Setup.}
One \sysa{} checkpoint fine-tuned on the clean RoboTwin demonstrations is
deployed on a single eighty gigabyte device under a sequence of cumulative
optimisations, then under four denoising iteration counts with all optimisations
active. Generation latency is the wall-clock time of one action chunk. The
worst-case gap is the longest interval between two consecutive commands when the
robot is driven at thirty hertz, which is the quantity a physical controller is
sensitive to. Success rate is measured on the clean RoboTwin suite so accuracy
and cost are read from the same runs. \tref{tab:cost} collects both panels.
